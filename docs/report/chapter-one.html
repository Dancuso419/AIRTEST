1.0Introduction

This project investigates the performance difference between WiFi 5 (IEEE 802.11ac) and WiFi 6 (IEEE 802.11ax) in high-density wireless environments, using the university lecture theatre as its setting. Lecture theatres are among the most demanding wireless environments on a campus: a single room may hold well over a hundred devices, all associated with the same access point, all contending for the same medium, and all active at the same moments — during a lecture stream, an in-class poll, or a mass download of course material.

Because no 802.11ac or 802.11ax hardware was available for controlled testing, the study is conducted entirely through discrete-event network simulation using NS-3, supported by an interactive dashboard that presents the resulting dataset. The simulation produces the evidence; the dashboard makes that evidence legible to a reader who is deciding whether an upgrade is justified.

This chapter establishes the background and motivation for the work, states the problem it addresses, sets out its aims and objectives, defines its scope and boundaries, records the risks identified and how they were managed, and describes how the remainder of the report is organised.

1.1Background and Motivation

The IEEE 802.11 family of wireless standards has, for most of its history, advanced by raising peak data rate. Each generation widened channels, added spatial streams, or increased modulation density. 802.11ac, ratified in 2013 and marketed as WiFi 5, followed that pattern: 80 and 160 MHz channels, up to eight spatial streams, and 256-QAM modulation.

802.11ax, marketed as WiFi 6, departs from it. Its headline additions are not primarily about making a single link faster; they are about making a crowded medium more efficient. [CITE] The principal mechanisms are:

  • OFDMA (Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access), which subdivides a channel into resource units so that several stations can be served within a single transmission opportunity rather than each contending for the whole channel in turn.
  • Improved MU-MIMO, extending multi-user transmission to the uplink as well as the downlink.
  • BSS colouring, which allows a station to identify transmissions belonging to a neighbouring basic service set and avoid deferring to them unnecessarily.
  • Target Wake Time (TWT), which schedules station wake periods to reduce contention and power consumption.

These are precisely the conditions a lecture theatre creates. The motivation for this project is therefore not the general question of whether WiFi 6 is a newer standard — it is — but the specific and practical question of whether its mechanisms deliver measurable benefit under the traffic and density that a lecture theatre actually produces, and if so, at what density that benefit begins.

1.2Problem Statement

Universities operating legacy WiFi 5 infrastructure experience well-documented symptoms in high-density teaching spaces: dropped associations, degraded video streaming, and rising latency during interactive activities such as live polling. WiFi 6 is presented by vendors as the remedy.

The difficulty is that the case for upgrading is typically made in terms of headline specifications rather than in terms of the environment the institution actually operates. An upgrade programme across a campus is a substantial capital commitment, and the institution rarely has quantified, environment-specific evidence on which to base it. Three questions in particular go unanswered:

  1. How large is the performance difference between the two standards, and how does it change as the number of active clients in a room increases?
  2. Which specific 802.11ax mechanisms account for any difference observed, as opposed to being assumed to contribute?
  3. At what client density does each standard cease to meet the demand placed on it?

This project addresses that gap by producing a controlled, reproducible comparison under lecture-theatre conditions, and by presenting the results in a form that a non-specialist decision-maker can interpret without misreading them.

1.3Aims and Objectives

The aim of this project is to quantify, through network simulation, the performance difference between WiFi 5 and WiFi 6 in a dense lecture-theatre environment, and to present the results in an interactive dashboard that communicates both the findings and their limitations.

The objectives are:

  1. To quantify performance differences between WiFi 5 and WiFi 6 as client density increases.
  2. To identify which WiFi 6 mechanisms — principally OFDMA, and inter-AP contention handling in the multi-AP case — account for any observed differences.
  3. To determine the practical saturation point, expressed as maximum supported clients, for each standard under realistic lecture-theatre traffic.

A supporting objective is to deliver a dashboard that reports these results without ever implying that a replayed measurement is a live one, and without offering the reader a scenario the simulation did not run.

1.4Project Scope

1.4.1Within scope

The study compares two standards, 802.11ac and 802.11ax, under three traffic profiles representative of lecture-theatre use: light interactive browsing, constant-bit-rate video streaming, and rate-limited bulk file transfer. Two topologies are simulated: a single access point, and a three-access-point deployment representing a larger theatre with overlapping coverage. Each combination is repeated across multiple randomised seeds so that run-to-run variability can be characterised rather than concealed.

Eight metrics are extracted from every run: aggregate throughput, per-user throughput, latency, jitter, packet loss, Jain's fairness index, airtime utilisation, and offered-load satisfaction ratio.

1.4.2Outside scope

  • No physical WiFi 5 or WiFi 6 hardware testing — none was available.
  • No live or on-demand simulation from the dashboard. The dashboard replays pre-computed results only.
  • No backend server, database, or user authentication; the dashboard is a static site.
  • No comparison with standards outside 802.11ac and 802.11ax.
  • Target Wake Time was excluded after verification established that it is not implemented in the NS-3 802.11ax module, and energy-efficiency claims were dropped with it rather than estimated.

1.4.3Density boundary

The original parameter matrix specified client densities up to 200. Timing measurements taken during implementation established that 802.11ax runs cost approximately five times their 802.11ac equivalents and rise sharply beyond a knee between sixteen and twenty clients, placing the upper densities beyond the compute available. The measured density range is therefore a constraint of the study and is reported as such in Chapter Five, not presented as a design choice.

1.6SWOT Analysis

Strengths

  • Full control over every variable; the two standards are compared under identical rooms, traffic, channel width and seeds.
  • Reproducible — every result traces to a recorded run with its own configuration metadata.
  • Automated verification of the analysis pipeline, independent of the simulator.

Weaknesses

  • No hardware validation; results are as accurate as the simulator's models.
  • Computational cost restricted the density range actually measured.
  • Short measurement windows inflate packet loss relative to a steady-state deployment.

Opportunities

  • Directly informs an institutional upgrade decision with environment-specific evidence.
  • The parameterised scenario extends to further densities, topologies and traffic profiles without redesign.
  • The dashboard is a reusable means of communicating network evidence to non-specialists.

Threats

  • Simulator defects can yield plausible but wrong figures, which are harder to detect than obvious failures.
  • Configuration choices materially affect outcomes and can understate a standard if set incorrectly.
  • Findings are specific to the modelled environment and do not generalise unconditionally.

1.7Significance of the Project

The project has two audiences. The primary audience is academic: the supervisor and examiners assessing the work as a final-year project. The secondary audience is practical: university IT and network planning staff who must decide whether, where, and when to invest in an 802.11ax upgrade.

For the second audience the significance lies in specificity. A vendor datasheet states what a standard can do under favourable conditions. This project states what it does under one institution's conditions, with the measurement method, the configuration, and the limitations all disclosed. A finding that an upgrade is not justified in a given room is as useful to that reader as a finding that it is, and the study is designed so that either outcome can be reported honestly.

Methodologically, the project also demonstrates a defensible approach to simulation-based evaluation: reconciliation checks that verify internal consistency of results, controlled ablation to attribute an observed effect to a specific mechanism, and an interface that discloses the boundaries of its own dataset rather than presenting an unbroken surface of numbers.
