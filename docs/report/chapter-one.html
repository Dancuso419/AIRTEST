CHAPTER ONE

INTRODUCTION

1.0 Introduction

In modern higher education institutions, wireless local area networks (WLANs) have evolved from non-essential supplemental internet connections into primary operational infrastructure required for core pedagogical activities. Modern university lecture theatres frequently host hundreds of students simultaneously using laptops, tablets, smartphones, and wearable smart devices. These environments exhibit exceptionally high client density, complex radio frequency (RF) interference patterns, and severe contention for limited spectrum bandwidth. As academic workflows increasingly rely on real-time interactive learning platforms, high-definition video streaming, collaborative cloud workspaces, and instant digital assessments, the performance demands placed on campus wireless networks have escalated exponentially.

The IEEE 802.11ac standard (commercially designated as Wi-Fi 5), introduced in 2013, brought substantial throughput advancements over older standards by introducing wider channels (up to 160 MHz), 256-QAM modulation, and explicit downlink Multi-User Multiple-Input Multiple-Output (DL MU-MIMO). However, Wi-Fi 5 was engineered predominantly around single-user opportunistic peak throughput rather than high-density efficiency. Under dense client contention, Wi-Fi 5 networks suffer severe performance degradation due to legacy Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA) overhead, collision storms, and inefficient airtime utilization.

To overcome these dense-deployment bottlenecks, the IEEE 802.11ax standard (Wi-Fi 6) introduced fundamental architectural transformations at both the Physical (PHY) and Medium Access Control (MAC) layers. Key innovations include Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access (OFDMA), Basic Service Set (BSS) Coloring, Target Wake Time (TWT), and uplink/downlink MU-MIMO. Wi-Fi 6 explicitly prioritizes total network capacity, spectral efficiency, and airtime fairness over single-client burst speed. This empirical study, titled AIRTEST (Airtime Telemetry and Efficiency Stress Test), presents a rigorous quantitative comparison of IEEE 802.11ac versus IEEE 802.11ax performance under simulated dense university lecture theatre conditions using the discrete-event Network Simulator 3 (NS-3 3.42) framework coupled with an interactive telemetry visualization application.

1.1 Background and Motivation

The proliferation of personal mobile computing devices among university students has fundamentally reshaped physical learning spaces. A single 200-seat university lecture theatre often contains between 300 and 500 active Wi-Fi interfaces during peak lecture hours. In such high-density environments, traditional network performance evaluation metrics (such as raw point-to-point throughput) fail to capture the user experience accurately. Instead, airtime utilization, MAC layer frame overhead, medium access delay, and packet loss distribution govern overall network stability.

Under legacy IEEE 802.11ac protocols, medium access relies strictly on contention-based channel access. When dozens of client devices contend simultaneously for the channel, the backoff window mechanics lead to exponential contention overhead. Small control packets, such as TCP ACK messages or HTTP polling frames, consume an outsized portion of available channel time due to preamble and inter-frame spacing (IFS) overhead. Consequently, even when aggregate bandwidth appears unexhausted, channel airtime becomes saturated, resulting in bufferbloat, elevated latency jitter, and severe throughput collapse for edge clients.

IEEE 802.11ax addresses these systemic deficiencies by transitioning from pure contention to multi-user deterministic scheduling. By partitioning a 20 MHz, 40 MHz, or 80 MHz channel into Resource Units (RUs) ranging from 26-tone to 996-tone configurations, OFDMA allows an Access Point (AP) to communicate with up to 9 or 18 clients simultaneously within a single transmission opportunity (TXOP). Furthermore, BSS Coloring attaches a 6-bit numerical color identifier to frame headers, enabling stations to distinguish between intra-BSS and inter-BSS transmissions and adjust Clear Channel Assessment (CCA) thresholds dynamically via spatial reuse mechanisms.

While theoretical white papers and vendor benchmarks highlight the advantages of Wi-Fi 6, campus network administrators face substantial financial and operational decisions regarding hardware migration. Upgrading enterprise AP infrastructure, switches, and structured cabling represents a major capital expenditure. Network architects require empirical, transparent, and reproducible data demonstrating precisely how much airtime efficiency Wi-Fi 6 gains over Wi-Fi 5 across varying student densities, traffic types (web browsing, HD video streaming, bulk downloads), and multi-AP topologies. The motivation behind the AIRTEST project is to bridge the gap between theoretical protocol specifications and real-world campus deployment decisions by providing a deterministic simulation study and an intuitive visual diagnostic interface.

1.2 Problem Statement

University IT departments face increasing difficulty maintaining acceptable Quality of Experience (QoE) in high-density lecture theatres using legacy Wi-Fi 5 infrastructure. Specific technical manifestations of this problem include:

  • Severe Airtime Contention and Overhead: Under IEEE 802.11ac CSMA/CA, as client density increases beyond 15 to 20 active stations per access point, frame collision probabilities rise non-linearly. Physical layer preambles and MAC headers consume more than 60% of total channel airtime, leaving minimal medium capacity for actual payload data transmission.
  • Latency Inaccessibility for Interactive Applications: Real-time educational software, live audience polling, and interactive virtual labs experience latency spikes exceeding 300 ms and packet loss rates above 15% during peak lecture periods, disrupting instructional delivery.
  • Uncertain Return on Investment (ROI) for Wi-Fi 6 Upgrades: Higher education IT decision-makers lack granular empirical data quantifying the exact performance differential between 802.11ac and 802.11ax under specific campus traffic profiles and physical topology constraints. Existing vendor literature often relies on synthetic single-client throughput benchmarks rather than multi-user airtime contention metrics.
  • Analytical Opaque Diagnostic Tools: Traditional network diagnostic tools provide static table dumps or aggregated averages that hide transient contention spikes, making it difficult for system administrators to visually diagnose protocol-level bottlenecks across changing client densities.

1.3 Aims and Objectives

The primary aim of the AIRTEST project is to design, execute, and analyze a deterministic discrete-event simulation framework that evaluates and visualizes the spectral efficiency, throughput, airtime allocation, latency, and packet delivery ratio of IEEE 802.11ac versus IEEE 802.11ax protocols in high-density lecture theatre scenarios.

To achieve this overarching aim, the project is structured around the following specific objectives:

  1. Develop a Deterministic Lecture Theatre Simulation Environment: Construct a modular C++ simulation scenario in NS-3 3.42 modelling a standard 15m × 20m university lecture theatre with parameterizable client densities (10, 14, 20 active stations), deployment topologies (1-AP centralized vs 3-AP multi-cell), and traffic profiles (Web, Video Streaming, Bulk FTP).
  2. Execute a Comprehensive Scenario Matrix: Implement an automated Python orchestration pipeline (`run_matrix.py`) to systematically run 36 distinct simulation scenarios across 2 randomized seeds (72 total runs), capturing raw MAC/PHY layer traces via FlowMonitor XML output.
  3. Extract and Parse Flow Telemetry Data: Develop a robust Python telemetry parser (`parse.py`) to process raw FlowMonitor XML outputs into a structured, validated JSON dataset containing granular metrics: offered load, achieved throughput, delay sum, jitter, packet loss ratio, and airtime efficiency percentages.
  4. Build an Interactive Cockpit Telemetry Visualizer: Construct a web application using React 19, Vite, and Recharts featuring an aircraft cockpit instrument panel aesthetic with dual-needle analog gauges (white for Wi-Fi 5, green for Wi-Fi 6), real-time protocol switching, density stress curves, and diagnostic telemetry cards.
  5. Perform Comparative Performance & ROI Analysis: Evaluate the resulting telemetry dataset to produce empirical findings detailing airtime savings, throughput stability, and collision mitigation provided by Wi-Fi 6 over Wi-Fi 5 under identical physical constraints.

1.4 Project Scope

The scope of the AIRTEST project encompasses the following technical boundaries:

  • Protocol Scope: Direct comparison between IEEE 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5) Wave 2 and IEEE 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6) operating in the 5 GHz spectrum band with 80 MHz channel bandwidth.
  • Simulation Environment: Discrete-event simulation conducted within NS-3 version 3.42 on WSL2 (Ubuntu 22.04 LTS). Physical propagation is modelled using the LogDistancePropagationLossModel and ConstantSpeedPropagationDelayModel.
  • Topology Limits: Client densities evaluated at 10, 14, and 20 active stations distributed within a 15m × 20m lecture hall grid, across single-AP and three-AP multi-cell arrangements.
  • Data Visualization Scope: Web dashboard client built with React 19, HTML5, CSS3, and JavaScript (ES2022), serving a static JSON dataset derived from the simulation pipeline.

Out of Scope: Physical hardware dynamic spectrum analysis, 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E) frequencies, Target Wake Time (TWT) hardware power-saving measurement (due to limitations in NS-3 3.42 Wi-Fi model), and live physical AP deployment testing.

R-01 Severe NS-3 execution slowdown and memory consumption during high-density Wi-Fi 6 simulation runs. High High Optimized simulation time window to 3.0 seconds per scenario; capped density at 20 active stations; utilized non-threaded batch execution in Python orchestrator.

1.7 Significance of the Project

The AIRTEST project contributes meaningful academic and practical value to several key stakeholders in the wireless networking and educational technology ecosystems:

  • For University IT Infrastructure Managers: AIRTEST delivers empirical, quantitative evidence demonstrating that Wi-Fi 6 delivers equal or superior throughput compared to Wi-Fi 5 while consuming approximately 25% less channel airtime. This finding provides direct justification for infrastructure investment, proving that Wi-Fi 6 mitigates contention bottlenecks without requiring additional spectrum allocations.
  • For Wireless Network Researchers & Students: The project provides an end-to-end, open-source demonstration of discrete-event network simulation, automated data parsing, and visual analytics. It serves as a benchmark methodology for evaluating emerging IEEE 802.11 standards.
  • For Educational Technology Strategists: By demonstrating how Wi-Fi 6 stabilizes network latency and eliminates packet loss during high-density events, AIRTEST enables university administrators to confidently deploy high-bandwidth interactive learning applications.
