Chapter One: Introduction

1.0 Introduction

This project presents a comparative performance analysis of IEEE 802.11ac (WiFi 5) and IEEE 802.11ax (WiFi 6) in high-density university lecture theatres. A lecture theatre is among the most demanding wireless environments on a campus: a single room may hold well over a hundred devices, all associated with the same access point, all contending for the same medium, and all active at the same moments — during a streamed lecture, an in-class poll, or a mass download of course material.

Because no 802.11ac or 802.11ax hardware was available for controlled testing at this scale, the study is conducted entirely through discrete-event network simulation using NS-3 version 3.42. The simulation produces the evidence; a static web dashboard, named AIRTEST, presents that evidence to a reader who must decide whether an upgrade is justified. The dashboard replays stored trials and never performs a live measurement, a distinction it states explicitly in its own interface.

This chapter establishes the background and motivation for the work, states the problem it addresses, sets out its aims and objectives, defines its scope, records the risks identified and how they were managed, presents a SWOT analysis, explains the significance of the work, and describes how the remainder of the report is organised.

1.1 Background and Motivation

For most of its history, the IEEE 802.11 family of standards advanced by raising peak data rate. Each generation widened channels, added spatial streams, or increased modulation density. IEEE 802.11ac, published in 2013 and marketed as WiFi 5, followed that pattern: 80 and 160 MHz channel bonding, up to eight spatial streams, 256-QAM modulation, and downlink multi-user MIMO.

IEEE 802.11ax departs from it. Its principal additions are not primarily concerned with making a single link faster; they are concerned with making a congested medium more efficient. The mechanisms of interest to this study are:

  • Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access (OFDMA). The channel is subdivided into resource units so that several stations can be served within a single transmission opportunity, rather than each contending for the whole channel in turn.
  • Bi-directional multi-user MIMO. Multi-user spatial multiplexing is extended to the uplink as well as the downlink.
  • BSS colouring. A numerical identifier in the PHY header allows a station to distinguish transmissions belonging to a neighbouring basic service set from its own, and to avoid deferring unnecessarily.
  • Target Wake Time (TWT). Station wake periods are scheduled to reduce contention and power consumption.

These are precisely the conditions a lecture theatre creates. The motivation for this project is therefore not the general question of whether WiFi 6 is newer — it is — but the specific and practical question of whether its mechanisms deliver measurable benefit under the traffic and density a lecture theatre actually produces, and if so, from what density onward.

1.2 Problem Statement

Universities operating legacy WiFi 5 infrastructure report well-documented symptoms in large teaching spaces: dropped associations, degraded video streaming, and rising latency during interactive activities such as live polling. WiFi 6 is marketed as the remedy.

The difficulty is that the case for upgrading is typically made in terms of headline specifications rather than in terms of the environment an institution actually operates. A campus-wide upgrade is a substantial capital commitment, and the institution rarely holds quantified, environment-specific evidence on which to base it. Three questions in particular remain unanswered:

  1. How large is the performance difference between the two standards, and how does it change as the number of active clients in a room increases?
  2. Which specific 802.11ax mechanisms account for any difference observed, rather than being assumed to contribute?
  3. At what client density does each standard cease to meet the demand placed upon it?

A secondary problem concerns presentation. Conventional network diagnostic output is a table of aggregated averages, which conceals both run-to-run variability and the conditions under which a figure was obtained. A decision-maker reading such a table cannot distinguish a robust difference from a sampling artefact.

1.3 Aims and Objectives

The aim of this project is to quantify, through discrete-event network simulation, the performance difference between WiFi 5 and WiFi 6 in a dense lecture-theatre environment, and to present the results in an interactive dashboard that communicates both the findings and their limitations.

The objectives are:

  1. To construct a parameterised lecture-theatre simulation scenario. A single C++ scenario in NS-3 3.42 modelling a seated lecture theatre, parameterised by standard, client density, traffic profile, access-point count and random seed.
  2. To execute a systematic scenario matrix. An automated Python orchestrator running every combination across multiple randomised seeds, with idempotent resumption and per-run fault isolation.
  3. To extract and validate flow telemetry. A Python parser converting FlowMonitor XML and PHY state traces into a single structured dataset, with internal reconciliation checks that detect inconsistent results rather than publishing them.
  4. To build an interactive comparison dashboard. A static web application presenting the dataset as paired instrument readings, with explicit provenance and disclosure of conditions the simulation did not cover.
  5. To quantify the difference and attribute it to mechanisms. To analyse the resulting dataset and identify which 802.11ax mechanisms account for the observed differences, using controlled ablation where a mechanism can be disabled independently.

1.4 Project Scope

The scope of the project is bounded as follows.

  • Protocol scope. IEEE 802.11ac (WiFi 5) against IEEE 802.11ax (WiFi 6), both in the 5 GHz band on an 80 MHz channel, with identical rate control.
  • Simulation environment. NS-3 version 3.42 under WSL2 (Ubuntu), using SpectrumWifiPhy with MultiModelSpectrumChannel, LogDistancePropagationLossModel for path loss and a Nakagami fading model.
  • Density and topology. Client densities of 10, 14, 20, 30 and 40 stations, in a single-access-point topology and a three-access-point topology.
  • Traffic profiles. Three profiles: light interactive browsing, constant-bit-rate video streaming, and rate-limited bulk transfer over TCP.
  • Presentation scope. A static web dashboard built with React 19 and Vite, consuming a pre-generated JSON dataset. No backend, database or authentication.

Out of scope: physical hardware testing; the 6 GHz band (WiFi 6E) and 802.11be (WiFi 7); Target Wake Time, which is not implemented in the NS-3 3.42 WiFi model and was therefore excluded rather than approximated; and live or on-demand simulation triggered from the dashboard.

Density boundary. The originally planned grid extended to 200 stations. Timing measurements taken during implementation established that 802.11ax runs cost approximately five times their 802.11ac equivalents and rise sharply beyond a knee between sixteen and twenty stations; a single 802.11ax run at 40 stations required approximately thirty minutes of wall-clock time for five seconds of simulated time. The upper densities were therefore unreachable within the compute available, and the measured range is reported as a constraint of the study rather than presented as a design choice.

1.7 Significance of the Project

The project has two audiences. The primary audience is academic: the supervisor and examiners assessing the work. The secondary audience is practical: university IT and network planning staff deciding whether, where and when to invest in an 802.11ax upgrade.

For the second audience the significance lies in specificity. A vendor datasheet states what a standard can do under favourable conditions. This project states what it does under one institution's conditions, with the measurement method, the configuration and the limitations all disclosed. A finding that an upgrade is not justified for a given room is as useful to that reader as a finding that it is, and the study is designed so that either outcome can be reported honestly.

Methodologically, the project demonstrates three practices that are transferable: reconciliation checks that verify the internal consistency of derived results before they are published; controlled ablation, in which a single mechanism is disabled to attribute an observed effect to it; and an interface that refuses to display a condition the simulation never ran.

2.0.1 Evolution of the IEEE 802.11 Standards

The 802.11 family has progressed through successive amendments, each raising the theoretical maximum throughput of a single link. 802.11n introduced MIMO and channel bonding to 40 MHz; 802.11ac extended bonding to 80 and 160 MHz, raised modulation to 256-QAM, and added downlink multi-user MIMO. Each of these advances improves the rate at which one station can be served (Bellalta, 2016; Chotalia & Gajjar, 2023).

802.11ax marks a change in objective. Where earlier amendments optimised for peak rate under favourable conditions, 802.11ax optimises for aggregate efficiency when many stations contend simultaneously — the condition under which earlier standards degrade most sharply. The amendment retains and slightly extends the physical-layer gains (1024-QAM) but concentrates on medium access (Khorov, Kiryanov, Lyakhov, & Bianchi, 2019; Afaqui, Garcia-Villegas, & Lopez-Aguilera, 2017).

2.0.2 High-Density Wireless Environments

A high-density environment is characterised not by traffic volume alone but by the number of stations contending for a shared medium. Under the distributed coordination function used by 802.11, each station that wishes to transmit must sense the medium idle and then wait a randomised backoff interval. As station count rises, the probability that two stations select overlapping backoff intervals rises with it, and the proportion of channel time consumed by collisions and retransmissions grows (Bellalta, 2016; Khorov et al., 2019).

Lecture theatres, conference halls and stadiums are the canonical examples. What distinguishes a lecture theatre is the synchronisation of demand: devices are not merely numerous but active at the same instants, because the activity is directed from the front of the room (Afaqui et al., 2017; Qu et al., 2019).

2.0.3 WiFi 6 Mechanisms: OFDMA, MU-MIMO, BSS Colouring and TWT

OFDMA. The channel is divided into resource units, and the access point schedules several stations within a single transmission opportunity. The gain is a reduction in per-frame overhead when many stations have small quantities of data to send; the corresponding cost is that any one station receives a fraction of the channel rather than the whole of it (Khorov et al., 2019; Bellalta, 2016). Simulation studies of OFDMA under contention-driven load report the same trade-off (Chotalia & Gajjar, 2023).

Multi-user MIMO. 802.11ax extends multi-user spatial multiplexing to the uplink, allowing several stations to transmit simultaneously under access-point coordination (Bellalta, 2016; Qu et al., 2019).

BSS colouring. Each basic service set is assigned a colour carried in the PHY header. A station receiving a frame with a foreign colour may apply a more permissive clear-channel assessment threshold, reducing unnecessary deferral in deployments where cells overlap (Wilhelmi, Barrachina-Muñoz, Cano, Selinis, & Bellalta, 2021; Wilhelmi, Barrachina-Muñoz, & Bellalta, 2019).

Target Wake Time. Stations negotiate scheduled wake intervals, reducing both power consumption and contention. TWT is not implemented in the NS-3 3.42 WiFi module and is therefore outside the scope of this study.

2.0.4 Network Simulation Tools and Discrete-Event Modelling

Where hardware is unavailable, evaluation proceeds by simulation. A discrete-event simulator maintains a queue of timestamped events and advances a virtual clock from one event to the next, so that simulated time is decoupled from wall-clock time. This permits exact control of every parameter and exact repeatability given a fixed random seed (Banks et al., 2010).

NS-3 is an open-source discrete-event network simulator with a detailed 802.11 model (Riley & Henderson, 2010; ns-3 Project, 2024), including physical-layer abstractions at both the YansWifiPhy and SpectrumWifiPhy levels. The latter models per-subband spectral density and is required for any scenario involving OFDMA, since resource-unit transmissions cannot be represented in a model that treats the channel as indivisible. Alternatives include OMNeT++ with the INET framework, which has also been extended to model 802.11ax (Inzillo & Ariza Quintana, 2025), and ns-2, now largely superseded (Riley & Henderson, 2010).

2.0.5 Performance Metrics for Wireless LAN Evaluation

The literature converges on a small set of metrics for evaluating WLAN performance, all of which are adopted here.

  • Aggregate and per-user throughput, in Mbps. Aggregate throughput measures the network; per-user throughput measures the experience of an individual.
  • Latency and jitter, in milliseconds. Jitter — the variation in delay — determines whether a stream can be played back smoothly.
  • Packet loss ratio, as a percentage of transmitted packets.
  • Jain's fairness index, in the range 0 to 1, quantifying how evenly capacity is distributed among stations (Jain, Chiu, & Hawe, 1984).
  • Airtime or channel utilisation, as a percentage of time the medium is occupied. This is the efficiency measure: two standards may deliver identical throughput while consuming very different amounts of channel time.
  • Offered-load satisfaction ratio, delivered throughput as a proportion of demand. This measures whether the network met what was asked of it, which is distinct from whether it was fast.

2.1 Related Works

2.1.1 Simulation-Based 802.11ax Studies

A body of simulation work evaluates 802.11ax mechanisms in isolation, typically comparing OFDMA-enabled and OFDMA-disabled configurations under synthetic traffic. Chotalia and Gajjar (2023) compare 802.11n, 802.11ac and 802.11ax in ns-3 and report throughput gains for 802.11ax under multi-station load. Work presented at the ns-3 workshop has validated the simulator's own OFDMA implementation against analytical prediction (WNS3, 2021), and subsequent analysis has extended that validation to contention-driven scenarios (PeerJ Computer Science, 2025). Such studies establish a mechanism's theoretical contribution but frequently model a single traffic type and a uniform station population.

2.1.2 Measurement Studies in High-Density Deployments

Where hardware is available, measurement studies report performance in real dense venues. These carry higher external validity than simulation but sacrifice control: the station population, background interference and traffic mix cannot be held constant between the two standards, so an observed difference cannot be attributed unambiguously to the standard (Afaqui et al., 2017; Qu et al., 2019). Comparable trade-offs are discussed in evaluations of spatial reuse, where measured gain depends heavily on the density and geometry of the deployment (Wilhelmi et al., 2021).

2.1.3 Interactive Network Performance Visualisation Tools

Tools for presenting network measurement to non-specialists range from consumer speed tests, which report a single instantaneous figure, to enterprise monitoring dashboards, which report time series for operators. Neither is designed to compare two protocol standards under matched conditions while disclosing the provenance and variability of each figure, which is the presentation problem this project addresses. Simulation studies conventionally report results as static plots in a paper (Chotalia & Gajjar, 2023; Wilhelmi et al., 2021) rather than as an artefact a reader can interrogate.

2.3 Summary

The literature establishes that 802.11ax was designed to improve efficiency under contention rather than peak rate, and identifies OFDMA, uplink multi-user operation and BSS colouring as the mechanisms responsible. It also establishes a settled set of evaluation metrics. What is less well covered is the combination this project addresses: a controlled comparison across several traffic profiles and densities, with the difference attributed to specific mechanisms by ablation, and the result presented in a form a non-specialist can act on without misreading it. Chapter Three sets out the methodology adopted to do so.

Chapter Three: Methodology

3.0 Overview

This chapter sets out how the project was carried out. The design is documented using standard UML notation for use case, activity and class-equivalent structure diagrams, together with structured-analysis notation for dataflow (Fowler, 2004). It states the research methodology, reviews four candidate software development models and justifies the one adopted, describes the fact-finding techniques used, specifies the tools and hardware, addresses ethical considerations, presents the requirements analysis and specification, and documents the system design through architecture, use case, data, activity, dataflow, control flow and interface diagrams.

3.1 Methodology

The project follows a quantitative, experimental methodology, structured according to established practice for discrete-event simulation studies (Banks, Carson, Nelson, & Nicol, 2010). The independent variables are the wireless standard, the client density, the traffic profile and the access-point count. The dependent variables are the eight performance metrics listed in §2.0.5. All other parameters — room geometry, propagation model, channel width, band, rate-control algorithm, simulated duration and random seed sequence — are held constant between the two standards, so that any measured difference is attributable to the standard and its mechanisms.

Each experimental cell is repeated across multiple random seeds. Reporting a single run would conflate the difference between standards with the variability of an individual run; repeating the cell allows the mean and the spread to be reported together, and allows a difference smaller than the spread to be identified as such rather than presented as a finding.

3.2 Development Method

Four development models were considered before the approach was fixed. The characterisations below follow the standard treatments in the software engineering literature (Sommerville, 2016; Pressman & Maxim, 2014). Each is described below with its characteristics, strengths and weaknesses, and its applicability to a project of this kind.

3.2.1 Waterfall Model

The Waterfall model organises development into sequential phases — requirements, design, implementation, verification, maintenance — in which each phase is completed and signed off before the next begins. The model is conventionally traced to Royce (1970), who presented the sequential diagram while simultaneously arguing that it is “risky and invites failure” without iteration between phases; the diagram was subsequently adopted more widely than the caution that accompanied it (Larman & Basili, 2003). Its defining property is that phases do not overlap and that requirements are fixed before design commences.

Its strengths are documentation and predictability (Sommerville, 2016). Because every phase produces a defined artefact before the next begins, progress is visible and the project's state at any moment is unambiguous. Where requirements are genuinely known in advance and unlikely to change — as in a regulated or contractual context — this produces an orderly and auditable process. It is also straightforward to plan and to teach, since responsibilities and deliverables map directly onto phases.

Its weaknesses follow from the same property (Pressman & Maxim, 2014). Because verification occurs only after implementation is complete, a defect in an early assumption is not discovered until late, when the cost of correcting it is highest. The model assumes requirements can be fully specified before any code is written, which is rarely true of research or exploratory work. It accommodates change poorly: a requirement discovered during implementation cannot be absorbed without returning to an earlier phase, and the model provides no mechanism for doing so.

For a simulation study, the decisive weakness is that correctness cannot be established by inspection. A simulation scenario that compiles, runs to completion and produces plausible numbers may nevertheless be measuring the wrong quantity. Under a strict Waterfall process, that class of defect would surface only in the verification phase, after the entire scenario matrix had been executed — at which point every result produced would be invalid and the compute expended would be unrecoverable. In this project the full matrix required several hours of computation, so discovering a measurement defect after it would have been a serious loss.

3.2.2 Incremental Model

The Incremental model divides the system into portions delivered in sequence, each adding function to a working whole (Sommerville, 2016). Larman and Basili (2003) record that incremental and iterative practice long predates its formalisation, with documented use in large engineering projects from the 1950s onward. Requirements are partitioned rather than fixed in advance, and each increment passes through its own design, implementation and test.

Its strength is that working software exists early and defects are found within the increment that introduced them. Risk is distributed across increments rather than concentrated at a single verification phase. Its weakness is that the architecture must be sound from the first increment; a structural error is expensive to correct once several increments depend on it. Effective partitioning also requires a good understanding of the system boundaries at the outset.

3.2.3 Iterative Model

The Iterative model builds an initial version of the whole system and refines it through repeated cycles, each revisiting requirements, design and implementation (Larman & Basili, 2003). Boehm’s (1988) spiral model formalises the approach by making risk assessment the criterion that governs each cycle. It differs from the Incremental model in that each cycle revises existing function rather than adding new function to a fixed plan.

Its strength is suitability to problems that are not fully understood at the start, since understanding is expected to change and each cycle incorporates what the previous cycle revealed. Its weaknesses are that the end point is harder to predict, that repeated revision can consume effort without converging, and that documentation tends to lag the implementation.

3.2.4 Prototype Model

The Prototype model builds a working but incomplete version of the system to elicit or validate requirements, which is then either refined into the product or discarded and rebuilt (Pressman & Maxim, 2014; Sommerville, 2016).

Its strength is in resolving uncertainty about requirements, particularly where users cannot articulate what they need until they see something concrete — which makes it well suited to interface design. Its weaknesses are that a prototype built for demonstration is rarely built for production, and that stakeholders may mistake a prototype for a nearly finished system and underestimate the work remaining.

3.3 Proposed Model

The model adopted for this project was incremental development structured around an initial end-to-end vertical slice, with prototyping applied specifically to the interface (Larman & Basili, 2003; Sommerville, 2016). This is a considered departure from a pure Waterfall process, and the justification is empirical rather than stylistic.

The defining risk of this project was not that the software would fail to run, but that it would run and produce numbers that were wrong in ways not visible on inspection. That risk cannot be managed by a process that defers verification until after implementation. Boehm’s (1988) principle — that each development cycle should address the greatest outstanding risk first — applies directly: the greatest risk here was measurement validity, so the first increment was made to exercise measurement end to end. It is managed by building one complete path through the system — a single simulation run, parsed, validated and displayed — and verifying that path end to end before extending it in any direction.

That decision is justified by its result. The vertical slice identified thirteen defects before the full scenario matrix was executed, of which the majority would have produced plausible but incorrect figures rather than visible failures. These are documented individually in §4.2. Under a sequential process, each would have been discovered only after the complete matrix had run, and every published figure would have required recomputation.

Prototyping was applied to the dashboard, where the requirement — that a non-specialist reader can interpret the comparison without misreading it — could not be specified in the abstract and had to be evaluated against something concrete.

Elements of Waterfall were retained where they were appropriate: the requirements and technical specification were written and fixed before implementation began, and the metric definitions were settled in advance so that they could not be adjusted to suit results after the fact. That last point is a matter of research integrity as much as process.

3.4.3 Data Analysis

Data analysis was the principal fact-finding technique and the source of every result reported. The four-stage structure below — trace extraction, per-run parsing, aggregation across replications, and derivation — follows standard practice for output analysis in discrete-event simulation, in which multiple independent replications are required before any statistic is reported (Banks et al., 2010). It proceeded in four stages. First, raw telemetry was extracted from each simulation run: a FlowMonitor XML trace of per-flow counters, a PHY state trace of channel occupancy, a run metadata record, and a time series sampled at fixed intervals of simulated time. Second, the traces were parsed into per-run metrics. Third, runs sharing a configuration were aggregated across seeds into a mean and standard deviation. Fourth, derived quantities — per-user throughput, satisfaction ratio, fairness index and interpolated maximum supported clients — were computed from the aggregates.

Reconciliation checks were applied at the second stage. The quantity transmitted must equal the quantity received plus the quantity lost; where the identity failed beyond a tolerance, the run was flagged rather than published. This check detected several defects described in §4.2.

3.6 Ethical Considerations

The project involves no human participants, no personal data and no live network capture. All traffic analysed is synthetic and generated within the simulator, so there is no risk to privacy and no requirement for ethical approval on those grounds.

The ethical obligations that do apply concern the integrity of the results, and were treated as binding constraints on both the analysis and the interface:

  • No fabricated data. No figure appears in the dataset or the dashboard that was not produced by a recorded simulation run.
  • No substitution. Where a requested combination was never simulated, the interface discloses this and offers nothing in its place. It does not display the nearest available scenario.
  • No manufactured contrast. Where the difference between the two standards is smaller than the variation between repeated runs, the result is reported as level rather than as a win for either standard.
  • No implied live measurement. The dashboard replays stored trials, and states so beside the readings rather than in a footnote.
  • Disclosure of limitations beside the figures. Metrics subject to known measurement artefacts carry that caveat at the point of reading.

3.7 Requirement Analysis

Requirements were derived from the objectives in §1.3 and from the constraints established during fact-finding. The analysis identified three subsystems with distinct responsibilities: a simulation subsystem that produces raw telemetry, an analysis subsystem that converts telemetry into validated metrics, and a presentation subsystem that communicates those metrics to a reader.

The decisive analytical finding was that the presentation subsystem must be able to represent absence. Because the study cannot cover the full parameter space, the interface must distinguish between a condition that was simulated and produced a result, and a condition that was never simulated — and must do so without inviting the reader to infer the missing value. This requirement shaped the control design described in §3.9.8.

A second finding was that the analysis subsystem must be verifiable independently of the simulator. Since simulator defects produce plausible outputs, correctness cannot be established by observing that the pipeline runs. The parser was therefore designed as a set of pure functions testable against fixtures with known correct answers.

3.9 System Design

3.9.1 Application Architecture

The system is a three-tier pipeline in which each tier communicates with the next through files on disk rather than through a live interface. This decoupling is deliberate: the simulation tier is expensive and runs rarely, the analysis tier is cheap and runs whenever the parser changes, and the presentation tier is a static artefact that can be deployed independently of both.

3.9.2 Use Case Diagram

The system has a single human actor. The simulation pipeline is modelled as a secondary actor because it supplies the data on which every use case depends, but it operates offline and is not invoked by the reader.

3.9.3 Data Design

3.9.8 User Interface Design

The interface was designed in code rather than in a mockup tool, and iterated against rendered output. Its organising metaphor is an instrument panel: each metric is a dial carrying two needles on one shared scale, so the difference between the standards is read directly from the gap between the needles rather than reconstructed by the reader from two separate figures.

Four design decisions follow from the requirements in §3.7:

  • Shared, fixed scales. A dial's full-scale value is fixed rather than derived from the data, so readings remain comparable between conditions. A dial that rescales itself defeats the purpose of an instrument.
  • Two needles of different lengths. Where the standards are identical, both needles remain visible; equal performance is a finding and must not be hidden by one needle occluding another.
  • Unpowered positions. A control position with no data behind it is rendered visibly dead and cannot be selected, disclosing the study's intended range without offering a value that was never measured.
  • Caveats at the point of reading. Metrics subject to known artefacts carry the caveat on the reverse of the dial, not in a footnote at the foot of the page.

3.10 Summary

This chapter established a quantitative experimental methodology in which the wireless standard is the only independent variable, reviewed four development models and justified incremental development around a verified vertical slice on the grounds that simulation defects produce plausible rather than visible failures, specified the tools and requirements, and documented the design of a three-tier pipeline whose tiers communicate through files. Chapter Four describes the implementation of that design and the testing applied to it.

Chapter Four: Implementation and Testing

4.0 Overview

This chapter describes what was built, the defects encountered during construction and how they were resolved, the testing strategy and its results, and how the delivered system is used. The account of defects is given in detail because it constitutes the substantive evidence that the results can be trusted: in a simulation study the principal threat is not visible failure but plausible error, and the value of the verification process lies in what it caught.

4.1 Main Features

4.1.1 Parameterised Simulation Scenario

A single C++ scenario models the lecture theatre and accepts every experimental variable as a command-line parameter: standard, traffic profile, client count, access-point count, seed, duration, sampling interval, and independent switches for downlink and uplink OFDMA. One scenario serving all configurations, rather than one file per condition, guarantees that the two standards traverse identical code paths for everything except the standard itself.

The three traffic profiles are defined as follows.

ProfileTransportOffered load per clientPayloadPattern
Browse (web)UDP0.5 Mbps mean512 BExponential on/off, mean 0.5 s
Stream (video)UDP3.0 Mbps1200 BConstant bit rate
Download (bulk)TCP5.0 Mbps1448 BContinuous, rate-limited

4.1.2 Matrix Orchestration

A Python orchestrator enumerates every parameter combination and executes runs across a worker pool. Two properties are load-bearing. It is idempotent: a run whose four output artefacts already exist is skipped, so an interrupted matrix resumes rather than restarting — which proved essential, as the matrix was interrupted and resumed several times. It is fault-isolating: a run that crashes or exceeds its timeout is recorded in a manifest with its exit status and standard error, and the batch continues.

4.1.3 Telemetry Extraction and Validation

The parser converts each run's four artefacts into validated metrics. Beyond arithmetic it performs reconciliation: transmitted packets must equal received plus lost, within a tolerance. A run failing this check is flagged in the dataset's warnings rather than published silently.

4.1.4 Instrument Cluster

Six dials present the metrics that describe an individual's experience and the network's cost. Each carries two needles on one fixed scale. Each dial turns on hover or keyboard focus to reveal what it measures, the direction that is better, its full-scale value, and — where applicable — the caveat that qualifies it.

4.1.5 Timed Replay

Engaging a condition replays the selected trial's recorded time series across fifteen seconds of playback. The needles move because the recorded data moves, not because an animation interpolates toward a final value. The transport states the playback rate relative to simulated time, computed from the measurement window rather than hard-coded, so the stated rate cannot drift from the truth.

4.1.6 Evaluation Report and Dataset Explorer

After a run completes, a report is available giving a plain-language verdict for each metric. Where the difference between standards falls within the run-to-run spread, the row is reported as level rather than as a win. The explorer plots any of the eight metrics against client density across the whole dataset, with a mean line and a standard-deviation band per standard.

4.2 Implementation Problems

Thirteen defects were identified during the vertical slice and subsequent development. Those with material consequences for the results are described below. The common characteristic is that almost none produced a visible failure; each produced numbers that looked reasonable.

4.2.1 WiFi 6 delivered no packets at all

The first WiFi 6 runs transmitted 112,480 packets and received none. The cause was the physical-layer model: YansWifiPhy has no per-subband spectrum representation and therefore drops high-efficiency multi-user PPDUs outright. OFDMA cannot be simulated on it at all.

4.2.2 TCP acknowledgement flows counted as payload

Bulk runs reported offered-load satisfaction of 431%. FlowMonitor records a separate flow for the reverse acknowledgement path of every TCP connection, and these were being counted as delivered payload. Ten clients produced twenty flows. An initial correction filtered by destination port lower bound only, which did not work because the acknowledgement flows carry an ephemeral destination port well above the application range.

4.2.3 Airtime utilisation inflated by one third

The PHY state trace was clamped to the measurement window at its start but not at its end, so channel occupancy recorded after the window closed was attributed to it.

4.2.4 Packet loss silently under-reported

FlowMonitor's own lostPackets counter reported zero for a flow that transmitted 937 packets and received 627. The counter does not account for packets still in flight when the monitor stops.

4.2.5 Simulator defects in the 802.11ax model

Two distinct defects in NS-3 3.42 itself were encountered. The first aborted with Unsupported Wi-Fi modulation class EHT — an 802.11be entity requested from an 802.11ax PHY — affecting two of seventy-two runs, seed-dependent. The second, arising only when uplink OFDMA was enabled, aborted with Not a Basic Trigger Frame in every multi-access-point WiFi 6 run without exception.

4.2.6 Further defects

Also identified and corrected: flow elements being matched from the classifier subtree as well as the statistics subtree; the measurement window including queue drain after traffic stopped; goodput computed from byte counts including headers; a hard-coded literal in the derived maximum-supported-clients output; a verdict generator producing "Infinity% more" when one standard delivered zero; a stream formatting flag persisting across fields and truncating every timestamp to its integer second; and replay animation that never started because the playback handler read a frame count of zero before the data reached state.

4.5 User Guide

The dashboard requires no installation. It is opened in a browser at the deployed address.

  1. Set the room. Choose the number of students, the activity, and the number of access points. A position rendered in outline with a hatched fill has no simulation behind it and cannot be selected.
  2. Engage. The stored trial for that condition is loaded and replayed over fifteen seconds. The transport reports playback rate relative to simulated time; it is always slower than the run, never faster.
  3. Read the instruments. Six dials, each with two needles on one scale: the dark needle is WiFi 5, the green needle is WiFi 6. Where they separate is the finding. Hovering or focusing a dial turns it over to reveal what it measures, which direction is better, and any caveat.
  4. Open the report. After the replay completes, "View report" gives a plain-language verdict for each metric. A row marked level means the difference is smaller than the variation between repeated runs.
  5. Explore the dataset. The lower section steps through all eight metrics — by arrow, by swipe, or with the left and right keys — plotting each against client density for the selected activity and topology.

Every reading carries a provenance line stating the seed, the trial index, the simulator version, and that the figure is pre-computed. No part of the interface performs a live measurement.

Chapter Five: Discussion, Conclusion and Recommendations

5.0 Overview

This chapter assesses the project against the objectives stated in §1.3, discusses the findings and what they mean for an institution considering an upgrade, records the limitations and challenges encountered, and sets out future enhancements and recommendations.

Provisional figures. The tables in this chapter reflect the dataset at the time of writing. Additional runs extending the density grid were still executing; confirm every figure against the final results.json before submission, and update the counts in §5.1 and §5.2 accordingly.

5.1 Objective Assessment

Objective 1 — Parameterised simulation scenario

Achieved. A single scenario accepts every experimental variable as a parameter, with downlink and uplink OFDMA independently switchable, and records its own configuration in each run's metadata.

Objective 2 — Systematic scenario matrix

Achieved. The orchestrator executed the matrix across multiple seeds with idempotent resumption and per-run fault isolation, both of which were exercised in practice.

Objective 3 — Telemetry extraction and validation

Achieved. The parser produces a validated dataset with reconciliation applied; 39 unit tests cover it, and the final dataset carries no warnings.

Objective 4 — Interactive dashboard

Achieved. The dashboard is deployed as a static site, presents both standards on shared scales, discloses provenance, refuses unsimulated conditions, and generates a plain-language evaluation.

Objective 5 — Quantification and mechanism attribution

Substantially achieved. The quantitative comparison is complete for the densities measured, and two mechanisms were attributed by controlled experiment rather than assumption.

The principal finding is that WiFi 6's advantage is conditional on contention and on workload. For streamed video on a single access point, WiFi 6 held lower latency and markedly lower jitter at every density measured:

At thirty students WiFi 6 held jitter to 0.44 ms where WiFi 5 reached 3.97 ms, a factor of nine, at identical delivered throughput. For a room streaming a lecture, jitter is the metric that determines whether playback is smooth, and this is the study's clearest result.

Two qualifications must accompany it. First, the efficiency advantage inverts between twenty and thirty students: below that point WiFi 6 delivers the same video using less airtime, and above it, more. WiFi 6 therefore buys its steadiness with channel time at higher densities rather than saving it. Second, for bulk transfer WiFi 5 was ahead on most measures at every density — OFDMA subdivides the channel, which is a poor fit for a single greedy flow per station.

Mechanism attribution. Two mechanisms were isolated by controlled experiment:

  • Uplink OFDMA and TCP. With uplink OFDMA disabled, bulk transfer at twenty stations achieved 88.8% of offered load with a fairness index of 0.92. Enabling it raised these to 97.7% and 0.99 — recovering nearly the whole deficit against WiFi 5's 100.1%. The mechanism is that TCP acknowledgements travel upstream, and with the uplink unscheduled each contends individually while the downlink is scheduled. The same change degraded latency for constant-bit-rate video, so the optimal configuration is workload-dependent, and it could not be enabled at all in multi-access-point scenarios owing to the simulator defect in §4.2.5.
  • BSS colouring. In three-access-point deployments WiFi 6 reduced packet loss from 6.9% to 1.6% at twenty stations under browsing traffic, an effect absent from single-access-point runs, which is consistent with colouring reducing unnecessary deferral between overlapping cells.

Not achieved: the saturation point. The third question posed in §1.2 — the density at which each standard ceases to meet demand — remains unanswered. Offered-load satisfaction remained at or above 94% at every density measured, and peak airtime reached only 71%. Neither standard saturated within the achievable grid, and the derived maximum supported clients is therefore correctly reported as a lower bound rather than a value.

5.2 Limitations and Challenges

  • No hardware validation. Every result depends on the fidelity of the NS-3 models. Nothing in this study has been corroborated against physical equipment.
  • Density ceiling. The grid reached forty stations against an intended two hundred. A single WiFi 6 run at forty stations required approximately thirty minutes of computation for five seconds of simulated time; the full intended grid was several orders of magnitude beyond the compute available. Conclusions do not extend to a full two-hundred-seat theatre.
  • No saturation observed. Following directly from the ceiling, the practical capacity limit of neither standard was reached.
  • Short measurement window. A three-second window inflates packet loss, because packets still queued when the window closes are counted as lost and that backlog grows with density.
  • Delivery-conditioned latency. Latency and jitter are computed over delivered packets only; packets never delivered would have been the slowest, so both understate delay where loss is non-trivial.
  • Simulator defects. Two defects in NS-3 3.42 constrained the study. The EHT abort affected isolated runs and was worked around by re-seeding. The uplink OFDMA trigger-frame abort prevented that configuration from being evaluated in any multi-access-point scenario, so the mechanism attribution for uplink OFDMA is limited to the single-access-point case.
  • Co-channel multi-AP model. All three access points share one channel, modelling a worst-case co-channel deployment rather than the channel plan a real installation would use.
  • Two seeds per cell. Sufficient to identify differences smaller than run-to-run variation, but a modest basis for a variance estimate.

5.3 Future Enhancements

  1. Extend the density grid to 60, 100 and 200 stations on hardware with substantially greater parallelism, to locate the saturation point that this study could not reach.
  2. Assign distinct channels to the three access points and compare against the co-channel case, isolating the contribution of channel planning from that of BSS colouring.
  3. Evaluate uplink OFDMA per workload once the simulator defect is resolved upstream, since the present evidence shows it helps TCP and harms constant-bit-rate video.
  4. Lengthen the measurement window to reduce the loss inflation and allow TCP congestion control to reach steady state.
  5. Increase seeds per cell to strengthen the variance estimate and narrow the band within which a difference is reported as level.
  6. Model mobility and association, since stations are currently fixed and pre-associated.
  7. Revisit Target Wake Time should a future NS-3 release implement it, restoring the energy-efficiency objective withdrawn in this study.

5.4 Recommendations

For an institution considering an 802.11ax upgrade, the evidence supports the following.

  • Prioritise rooms whose dominant traffic is streamed content. That is where the measured benefit lies: markedly lower jitter at equal throughput, sustained across every density measured.
  • Do not expect higher throughput. Neither standard saturated, and delivered throughput was equivalent throughout. The benefit is consistency, not speed.
  • Do not upgrade small rooms on this evidence. Below roughly twenty simultaneously active stations the advantage is small, and for bulk transfer WiFi 5 performed better.
  • Plan channels deliberately in multi-AP rooms. The multi-AP results here model a worst-case co-channel deployment; BSS colouring reduced loss substantially under those conditions, but channel planning addresses the same problem directly.
  • Treat these figures as indicative, not predictive. They are simulation results for one modelled room at densities up to forty stations, and should inform a pilot deployment rather than replace one.

5.5 Summary

The project delivered a parameterised simulation scenario, an orchestrated scenario matrix, a validated analysis pipeline and a deployed comparison dashboard, and used them to quantify the difference between WiFi 5 and WiFi 6 under lecture-theatre conditions. Four of five objectives were fully achieved and the fifth substantially so, with the saturation point remaining out of reach on the compute available.

The central conclusion is that WiFi 6's advantage is conditional rather than universal. It is real, mechanistically explicable and largest where the workload consists of many stations sending small frames at the same time — the streamed-lecture case — where it held jitter nine times lower than WiFi 5 at thirty students. It is absent, and sometimes reversed, for bulk transfer and at low density, and above twenty students it is bought with additional channel time rather than saved. An institution acting on this evidence should upgrade the rooms that stream, and should not expect the upgrade to make downloads faster.
